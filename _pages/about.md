    <h2>Working Papers</h2>

    <!-- JOB MARKET PAPER -->
    <details class="paper-card paper-featured" open>
      <summary class="paper-summary">
        <span class="paper-title">
          Headline Selection and Misleading Information
        </span>
        <span class="paper-badge">Job Market Paper</span>
        <span class="paper-status">
          Draft available upon request
        </span>
      </summary>

      <div class="paper-abstract">
        <p>
          Online news markets separate the headline from the article it
          represents: the headline circulates freely, while access to the
          article may require payment. Because many readers consume news
          through headlines alone, the headline is often the only part of
          the news they ever see. I develop a model in which a
          profit-maximizing outlet observes a truthful article, publishes
          one of its components as the headline, and charges for access to
          the rest. The outlet selects the component that leaves readers
          with the most residual uncertainty about the article's conclusion.
          In equilibrium, this mechanism generates misleading headlines:
          truthful headlines that induce rational headline-only readers to
          take actions contrary to those they would take after reading the
          full article. The model predicts that more informative articles
          have more informative headlines, that greater internal
          heterogeneity reduces headline–article alignment, and that this
          disclosure rule is invariant to whether readership is monetized
          through subscriptions or advertising. I evaluate these predictions
          using two corpora comprising more than 440,000 headline–article
          pairs from major English-language newspapers. A scalable semantic
          procedure measures informativeness, article heterogeneity, and
          headline–article alignment. In the cross-section, more informative
          articles have more informative headlines, while more heterogeneous
          articles have more specific but less aligned headlines. A
          difference-in-differences analysis of the 2011
          <em>New York Times</em> paywall finds no change in alignment
          relative to <em>The Guardian</em>. Together, the results identify
          a source of media bias arising from the truthful but strategic
          selection of headlines.
        </p>
      </div>
    </details>

    <!-- EDITORIAL PLACEMENT -->
    <details class="paper-card">
      <summary class="paper-summary">
        <span class="paper-title">
          Pick or Make? Editorial Placement and the Demand for News
        </span>
        <span class="paper-status">Draft coming soon</span>
      </summary>

      <div class="paper-abstract">
        <p>
          Articles at the top of a newspaper's homepage are read far more
          than the rest. Do editors pick winners, placing at the top the
          articles readers would have read anyway, or does placement make
          them? We combine a year of daily data on the <em>New York Times</em>,
          covering every article published, the daily lists of most read and
          most shared articles, and hourly snapshots of the homepage, and
          compare the same article with itself across days as editors move
          it around the page. Moving an article into the top third of the
          homepage raises its probability of reaching the most-read list
          roughly tenfold; editors' selection of appealing articles accounts
          for at most 0 to 7 percent of this premium, and editors mildly
          demote articles after they are read rather than promote them.
          A rank-ordered logit estimated on a valid set of alternatives
          confirms the magnitude and shows that placement matters far more
          than content, and more for reading than for sharing. The appeal
          of a new article is predictable out of sample, mainly through its
          topic, yet editors respond to that signal only weakly.
          Counterfactual homepages show that the homepage decides about half
          of the most-read list, and that a click-maximizing allocation of
          the same slots would favor day-old US politics over the fresh
          international and business news editors place at the top, raising
          the appeal of what is read by about one standard deviation of
          article length. Among the articles a newspaper publishes, editors
          largely decide which are read, and they use that power for
          freshness and importance rather than for clicks.
        </p>
      </div>
    </details>

    <!-- RIOTS AND FISCAL TRANSFERS -->
    <details class="paper-card">
      <summary class="paper-summary">
        <span class="paper-title">
          Civil Rights Riots and Fiscal Transfers in the U.S.
        </span>
        <span class="paper-coauthors">
          with
          <a
            href="https://sites.google.com/view/mgeiras/inicio"
            target="_blank"
            rel="noopener noreferrer"
          >Martin Gonzalez-Eiras</a>
        </span>
      </summary>

      <div class="paper-abstract">
        <p>
          How do governments adjust the allocation of resources in the face
          of civil unrest? We study the fiscal response to the U.S. 1960s
          racial riots, mapping the near-universe of race-related civil
          disturbances to annual Community Action Program (CAP) transfers
          at the commuting zone (CZ) level. We find that riot severity—not
          mere occurrence—is associated with an increase in transfers to
          the affected CZ the following fiscal year, a result robust across
          empirical specifications. Greater riot severity in neighboring,
          same-state, CZs reduces a CZ's own transfers, suggesting that
          government's reaction to unrest thus took the form of a
          reallocation of antipoverty funds across poor communities. To
          disentangle whether the relation between rioting activity and
          transfers is direct, or mediated through media coverage of these
          events, we add a measure of media reporting and find that its
          effect is negligible.
        </p>
      </div>
    </details>

    <!-- CURSE OF POPULARITY -->
    <details class="paper-card">
      <summary class="paper-summary">
        <span class="paper-title">
          The Curse of Popularity: Endogenous Agendas and Media Bias
        </span>
      </summary>

      <div class="paper-abstract">
        <p>
          I develop a model where media outlets jointly choose which topic
          to cover (agenda setting) and how to report (editorial slant)
          under limited consumer attention. I characterize the conditions
          under which a “curse of popularity” emerges: when a topic becomes
          sufficiently popular, competing outlets converge on that same
          issue and—rather than conveying the most informative coverage—have
          incentives to introduce editorial bias, crowding out other topics
          and reducing welfare. By contrast, when attention is more
          dispersed, outlets tend to specialize across topics and (in
          equilibrium) report more truthfully, improving informational
          quality and decision-making.
        </p>
      </div>
    </details>
