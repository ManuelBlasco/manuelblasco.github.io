**I am a Ph.D. Candidate in Economics at the University of Bologna and a candidate on the 2025-2026 Job Market.**

I am a microeconomist specializing in **Media Economics** and **Information Economics**. My research focuses on developing theoretical models to understand how digital media incentives shape the supply and demand for information, and consequently, its quality.

To test my hypotheses empirically, I combine my theoretical framework with modern **Text Analysis** techniques. Recently, I have also expanded my research agenda into **Experimental Economics**.

## Research

### Media Economics

<details>
  <summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: bold; font-size: 1.1em;">
    Headline selection and misleading information (Job Market Paper)
  </summary>
  <div style="margin-top: 10px; margin-left: 20px; font-style: italic;">
    <p>
      I develop a model in which news outlets choose headlines to maximize the “information gap” relative to the full article and thereby stimulate reading. A central result is that economic incentives alone can endogenously generate belief-reversing headlines, even in the absence of ideological bias and with hard information constraints. Using a novel dataset of 100,000 U.S. news articles and LLM-based text analysis, I show that headline–content divergence increases with an article’s semantic heterogeneity, decreases with its informativeness, and that a non-negligible share of headlines meaningfully misrepresent the article’s main message.
    </p>

<details>
  <summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: bold; font-size: 1.1em;">
    The Scope Wedge: How Private Attention and Public Signaling Diverge in News Demand
  </summary>
  <div style="margin-top: 10px; margin-left: 20px; font-style: italic;">
    <p>
     I use a unique NYT dataset in which each article appears simultaneously in two attention rankings—Most Viewed and Most Shared—to study how private and public use of news diverge for identical content. I combine these empirical patterns with a simple model of informational and signaling utility. Preliminary results show a consistent “scope wedge”: locally useful articles attract more private attention, while nationally framed and identity-related pieces are disproportionately shared. Moreover, contrary to common claims, shared articles are not simpler—they appear more sophisticated and moralizing, consistent with signaling rather than “dumbing down.”
    </p>
