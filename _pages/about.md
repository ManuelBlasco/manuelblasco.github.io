**I am a Ph.D. Candidate in Economics at the University of Bologna. I will be on the 2026-2027 job market.**

I am a microeconomist working on Media and Information Economics and Political Economy. My research agenda examines how economic incentives in digital media shape the supply, demand, and quality of information. I develop theoretical models and test their predictions using large-scale text data and computational text analysis to identify key mechanisms behind information choice and attention.

### Working Papers


<details open style="padding: 10px 12px; border: 1px solid #e5e5e5; border-radius: 10px; margin: 0.9rem 0;">
  <summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.05em;">
    ⭐ Headline Selection and Misleading Information
    <span style="display:inline-block; margin-left:8px; padding:2px 8px; border-radius:999px; font-size:0.8em; font-weight:700; border:1px solid #c9c9c9;">
      Job Market Paper
    </span>
    <span style="display:inline-block; margin-left:8px; font-size:0.85em; font-weight:400; color:#666;">
      Draft available upon request
    </span>
  </summary>
  <div style="margin-top:10px; margin-left:18px;">
    <p>
      Online news markets separate the headline from the article it represents: the
      headline circulates freely, while access to the article may require payment. Because
      many readers consume news through headlines alone, the headline is often the only
      part of the news they ever see. I develop a model in which a profit-maximizing outlet
      observes a truthful article, publishes one of its components as the headline, and
      charges for access to the rest. The outlet selects the component that leaves readers
      with the most residual uncertainty about the article's conclusion. In equilibrium,
      this mechanism generates misleading headlines: truthful headlines that induce
      rational headline-only readers to take actions contrary to those they would take
      after reading the full article. The model predicts that more informative articles have
      more informative headlines, that greater internal heterogeneity reduces
      headline--article alignment, and that this disclosure rule is invariant to whether
      readership is monetized through subscriptions or advertising. I evaluate these
      predictions using two corpora comprising more than 440,000 headline--article pairs
      from major English-language newspapers. A scalable semantic procedure measures
      informativeness, article heterogeneity, and headline--article alignment. In the
      cross-section, more informative articles have more informative headlines, while more
      heterogeneous articles have more specific but less aligned headlines. A
      difference-in-differences analysis of the 2011 <em>New York Times</em> paywall finds
      no change in alignment relative to <em>The Guardian</em>. Together, the results
      identify a source of media bias arising from the truthful but strategic selection of
      headlines.
    </p>
  </div>
</details>

<details style="margin: 0.9rem 0; padding: 0.25rem 0;">
<summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.05em;">
Pick or Make? Editorial Placement and the Demand for News
<span style="font-size: 0.8em; font-weight: 400; color: #888; margin-left: 8px;">
(Draft — Coming Soon)
</span>
</summary>
<div style="margin-top:10px; margin-left:18px;">
<p>
Articles at the top of a newspaper's homepage are read far more than the rest. Do editors pick winners, placing at the top the articles readers would have read anyway, or does placement make them? We combine a year of daily data on the \emph{New York Times}, covering every article published, the daily lists of most read and most shared articles, and hourly snapshots of the homepage, and compare the same article with itself across days as editors move it around the page. Moving an article into the top third of the homepage raises its probability of reaching the most-read list roughly tenfold; editors' selection of appealing articles accounts for at most 0 to 7 percent of this premium, and editors mildly demote articles after they are read rather than promote them. A rank-ordered logit estimated on a valid set of alternatives confirms the magnitude and shows that placement matters far more than content, and more for reading than for sharing. The appeal of a new article is predictable out of sample, mainly through its topic, yet editors respond to that signal only weakly. Counterfactual homepages show that the homepage decides about half of the most-read list, and that a click-maximizing allocation of the same slots would favor day-old US politics over the fresh international and business news editors place at the top, raising the appeal of what is read by about one standard deviation of article length. Among the articles a newspaper publishes, editors largely decide which are read, and they use that power for freshness and importance rather than for clicks.
</p>
</div>
</details>

### Work in Progress


<details style="margin: 0.9rem 0; padding: 0.25rem 0;">
<summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.05em;">
Civil Right Riots and Fiscal Transfers in the U.S. (with
<a href="https://sites.google.com/view/mgeiras/inicio" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="font-weight: 700;">
Martin Gonzalez-Eiras
</a>)
</summary>
<div style="margin-top:10px; margin-left:18px;">
<p>
How do governments adjust the allocation of resources in the face of civil unrest? We
study the fiscal response to the U.S. 1960s racial riots, mapping the near-universe of
race-related civil disturbances to annual Community Action Program (CAP) transfers
at the commuting zone (CZ) level. We find that riot severity—not mere occurrence—is
associated with an increase in transfers to the affected CZ the following fiscal year,
a result robust across empirical specifications. Greater riot severity in neighboring,
same-state, CZs reduces a CZ’s own transfers, suggesting that government’s reaction to
unrest thus took the form of a reallocation of antipoverty funds across poor communities.
To disentangle whether the relation between rioting activity and transfers is direct, or
mediated through media coverage of these events, we add a measure of media reporting
and find that its effect is negligible.
</p>
</div>
</details>

<details style="margin: 0.9rem 0; padding: 0.25rem 0;">
  <summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.05em;">
    The Curse of Popularity: Endogenous Agendas and Media Bias
  </summary>
  <div style="margin-top:10px; margin-left:18px;">
    <p>
      I develop a model where media outlets jointly choose which topic to cover (agenda setting) and how to report (editorial slant) under limited consumer attention. I characterize the conditions under which a “curse of popularity” emerges: when a topic becomes sufficiently popular, competing outlets converge on that same issue and—rather than conveying the most informative coverage—have incentives to introduce editorial bias, crowding out other topics and reducing welfare. By contrast, when attention is more dispersed, outlets tend to specialize across topics and (in equilibrium) report more truthfully, improving informational quality and decision-making.
    </p>
  </div>
</details>
